# pybullet_busyboard assets

Renders and annotated oracle-solve trajectories for the `pybullet_busyboard` domain.
The robot faces a children's busy board: a row or two of PartNet-Mobility toggle switches wired to a row of lamps by a relation it never observes.
A lamp lights only after its drive condition has held for a while, so the evidence for what a button did arrives long after the button was pressed.

## What the board hides

Each lamp has a **driver** button and, often, an **enabler** that must be on as well.
That conjunction is the interlock, and it is the structure prior busyboard environments for robot learning explicitly leave out: BusyBot (Liu, Xu and Song, CoRL 2022) states in Sec. 3 that it excludes many-to-one relations "to eliminate possible ambiguities".
Including them is the point, since an interlock is exactly what undirected play confounds and a chosen experiment separates.

Behind each lamp sits a hidden `charge` that accumulates while the lamp is driven and bleeds away faster when it is not.
The observable `brightness` is flat at zero through the whole early accumulation and only then ramps, so the charge is genuinely latent rather than merely rescaled.
Some buttons drive nothing at all.

## Init-state renders

`init_<B>buttons_<L>lamps.png`, generated by `docs/envs/busyboard/render_busyboard_media.py` (900x620, canonical env camera).

Buttons are the dark switches with pale sliders at the front; which side a slider sits on is that button's state.
Lamps are the blocks behind them, grey when dark and amber when lit, interpolating in between.
Boards of three buttons appear in one row; larger boards use a second row, which the push skill reaches at least as reliably as the front one.

## Annotated videos

All four clips run on the same four-button, three-lamp test board, so any difference between them is attributable to the policy rather than the board.
That board's wiring is `lamp0 <- button0 AND button2`, `lamp1 <- button0 AND button1`, `lamp2 <- button0 AND button3`: `button0` is a shared enabler for all three lamps, and each lamp has its own second button.
The goal asks for `lamp0` and `lamp2` lit and `lamp1` dark.

Each frame pairs the camera view an agent actually gets with a panel showing the board's hidden state - the true wiring and every lamp's latent charge.
The red mark on each charge bar is the brightness onset; left of it the lamp looks completely dead.

### Outcomes

- `oracle_solve.mp4` (11 s) - press `button0`, `button2`, `button3`, then wait. Solves the task.
- `press_everything_fails.mp4` (12 s) - latch every button and wait, the policy an agent falls into once it knows buttons matter but not which. It lights every lamp it can, including `lamp1`, and fails the off-target.

The second clip is the reason goals require some lamp to stay dark.
Without an off-target, latching every button would solve every task without knowing anything about the board.

### Mechanism

These two show why the board is hard, rather than only what happens on it.

- `interlock.mp4` (20 s) - press `button2` alone, one of the two buttons `lamp0` needs, and wait. Nothing happens for ten full seconds, and the charge bar makes clear it is not a slow response: the bar never leaves zero, because the drive condition was never met. Press `button0` as well and the charge climbs and the lamp lights. This conjunction is the many-to-one relation that prior busyboard environments for robot learning exclude by design.
- `delayed_credit.mp4` (17 s) - the confound. `button0` and `button2` are pressed, so `lamp0` begins charging invisibly; then an unrelated `button1` is pressed, and `lamp0` lights part-way through that press. The button that appears to cause the effect is not the button that caused it, and the charge bar is the only thing in the frame that says so. This is the mis-attribution a press-and-look agent makes, and the reason exploration here has to be designed rather than merely thorough.
