# pybullet_busyboard assets

Renders and annotated oracle-solve trajectories for the `pybullet_busyboard` domain.
The robot faces a children's busy board: a row or two of PartNet-Mobility toggle switches wired to a row of lamps by a relation it never observes.
A lamp lights only after its drive condition has held for a while, so the evidence for what a button did arrives long after the button was pressed.

## What the board hides

Each lamp has a **driver** button and an **enabler** that must be on as well (every lamp, at the default `busyboard_interlock_prob = 1.0`).
That conjunction is the interlock, and it is the structure prior busyboard environments for robot learning explicitly leave out: BusyBot (Liu, Xu and Song, CoRL 2022) states in Sec. 3 that it excludes many-to-one relations "to eliminate possible ambiguities".
Including them is the point, since an interlock is exactly what undirected play confounds and a chosen experiment separates.

Behind each lamp sits a hidden `charge` that accumulates while the lamp is driven and bleeds away faster when it is not.
The observable `brightness` is flat at zero through the whole early accumulation and only then ramps, so the charge is genuinely latent rather than merely rescaled.
Some buttons drive nothing at all.

## Init-state renders

`init_<B>buttons_<L>lamps.png`, generated by `docs/envs/busyboard/render_busyboard_media.py` (900x620, canonical env camera).

Buttons are the coloured switches with pale sliders at the front; which side a slider sits on is that button's state.
Every button and lamp has a fixed colour that is also a `color` feature in the state: buttons are red, green, blue, orange and purple in index order, lamps yellow, cyan and magenta.
The red button is the same control on every board, so a rule about it means the same thing at every board size.
Lamps are the blocks behind the buttons, grey when dark and glowing in their own colour when lit, interpolating in between; each bulb stands on a base in the lamp's colour, which identifies it while it is dark.
Boards of three buttons appear in one row; larger boards use a second row, which the push skill reaches at least as reliably as the front one.

## Task cards

`task_<split>_<idx>.png`, from the same script: one seed-0 task per distinct board size and goal, the initial board with the goal and the oracle's press set written underneath.
The cards are rendered with 6 train and 12 test tasks so that every goal type appears; the wiring is the same at any task count, only the goal draw differs.
The two training answers (red + green for the cyan lamp, green + blue for the yellow lamp) are also the answers on every test board, which is the point of the split.

## Train / test split

Training boards have three buttons and two lamps; test boards have four or five buttons and two or three lamps.
The wiring is fixed per seed and every test board extends the training board: the yellow and cyan lamps keep exactly their training drive conditions, and the buttons a test board adds are either decoys or drive the new magenta lamp.
Goals only ever ask for the magenta lamp to stay dark, so a rule learned in training stays true at test and the new buttons are ones to leave alone.

## Annotated videos

All four clips run on the same four-button, three-lamp test board, so any difference between them is attributable to the policy rather than the board.
That board's wiring is `yellow lamp0 <- green button1 AND blue button2`, `cyan lamp1 <- red button0 AND green button1`, `magenta lamp2 <- red button0 AND orange button3`.
The green button is shared between the yellow and cyan lamps, and the red button between the cyan and magenta lamps.
The goal asks for the cyan lamp lit and the yellow and magenta lamps dark.

Each frame pairs the camera view an agent actually gets with a panel showing the board's hidden state - the true wiring and every lamp's latent charge.
The red mark on each charge bar is the brightness onset; left of it the lamp looks completely dead.

### Outcomes

- `oracle_solve.mp4` (10 s) - press the red and green buttons, then wait. Solves the task.
- `press_everything_fails.mp4` (12 s) - latch every button and wait, the policy an agent falls into once it knows buttons matter but not which. It lights every lamp it can, including the magenta one, and fails the off-target.

The second clip is the reason goals require some lamp to stay dark.
Without an off-target, latching every button would solve every task without knowing anything about the board.

### Mechanism

These two show why the board is hard, rather than only what happens on it.

- `interlock.mp4` (20 s) - press the blue button alone, one of the two buttons the yellow lamp needs, and wait. Nothing happens for ten full seconds, and the charge bar makes clear it is not a slow response: the bar never leaves zero, because the drive condition was never met. Press the green button as well and the charge climbs and the lamp lights. This conjunction is the many-to-one relation that prior busyboard environments for robot learning exclude by design.
- `delayed_credit.mp4` (17 s) - the confound. The green and blue buttons are pressed, so the yellow lamp begins charging invisibly; then the unrelated orange button is pressed, and the yellow lamp lights part-way through that press. The button that appears to cause the effect is not the button that caused it, and the charge bar is the only thing in the frame that says so. This is the mis-attribution a press-and-look agent makes, and the reason exploration here has to be designed rather than merely thorough.
