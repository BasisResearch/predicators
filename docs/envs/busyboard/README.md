# pybullet_busyboard assets

Renders and annotated oracle-solve trajectories for the `pybullet_busyboard` domain.
The robot faces a children's busy board: a row or two of PartNet-Mobility toggle switches wired to a row of lamps by a relation it never observes.
A lamp lights only after its drive condition has held for a while, so the evidence for what a button did arrives long after the button was pressed.

## What the board hides

Each lamp has a **driver** button and up to two **enablers** that must be on as well (every lamp has at least one at the default `busyboard_interlock_prob = 1.0`; half of those have two, `busyboard_double_enabler_prob = 0.5`).
That conjunction is the interlock, and it is the structure prior busyboard environments for robot learning explicitly leave out: BusyBot (Liu, Xu and Song, CoRL 2022) states in Sec. 3 that it excludes many-to-one relations "to eliminate possible ambiguities".
Including them is the point, since an interlock is exactly what undirected play confounds and a chosen experiment separates.
Half of the lamps also have an **inhibitor** (`busyboard_inhibitor_prob = 0.5`): a button that must stay off, so "press everything" never solves anything.

The board has memory.
A lamp is **armed** only when its driver is pressed while every enabler is already on, and disarmed when the driver goes off (`busyboard_latch`).
The same buttons pressed in the other order light nothing until the driver is released and pressed again, so the board is not a function of the button setting and a table of settings cannot describe it.

Driving more than `busyboard_breaker_limit = 2` lamps at once trips the **breaker**: every charge drops to zero, nothing charges until every button has been released, and the green tile between the buttons and the lamps turns red.

Behind each lamp sits a hidden `charge` that accumulates while the lamp is driven and bleeds away faster when it is not.
The observable `brightness` is flat at zero through the whole early accumulation and only then ramps, so the charge is genuinely latent rather than merely rescaled.
Under partial observability the latch is hidden too; fully observed, it is the lamp's `armed` feature.
Some buttons drive nothing at all.

## Init-state renders

`init_<B>buttons_<L>lamps.png`, generated by `docs/envs/busyboard/render_busyboard_media.py` (900x620, canonical env camera).

Buttons are the coloured switches with pale sliders at the front; which side a slider sits on is that button's state.
Every button and lamp has a fixed colour that is also a `color` feature in the state: buttons are red, green, blue, orange, purple, black, brown and grey in index order, lamps yellow, cyan, magenta and white.
The red button is the same control on every board, so a rule about it means the same thing at every board size.
Lamps are the blocks behind the buttons, grey when dark and glowing in their own colour when lit, interpolating in between; each bulb stands on a base in the lamp's colour, which identifies it while it is dark.
Four buttons fit in a row, so every board here uses a second row, which the push skill reaches at least as reliably as the front one.

## Task cards

`task_<split>_<idx>.png`, from the same script: one seed-0 task per distinct board size and goal, the initial board with the goal and the oracle's press set written underneath.
The cards are rendered with 6 train and 12 test tasks so that every goal type appears; the wiring is the same at any task count, only the goal draw differs.
Every training answer is also the answer for that lamp on every test board, which is the point of the split; a test goal composes two or three of them.

## Train / test split

Training boards have four buttons and three lamps; test boards have seven or eight buttons and four lamps.
The wiring is fixed per seed and every test board extends the training board: the yellow, cyan and magenta lamps keep exactly their training drives, and the buttons a test board adds are decoys, drive the new white lamp, or inhibit a training lamp (`busyboard_extension_inhibitor_prob = 0.5`).
A rule learned in training therefore stays true at test as long as the new buttons stay off; pressing them, as probing every button does, can break a lamp the goal needs.
Test goals may ask for the white lamp to be lit (`busyboard_test_extension_lit`), so the agent has to find a condition among buttons it never saw, and ask for at least two lamps lit (`busyboard_min_lit_test = 2`, against 1 for training), so solving one composes learned conditions in the right press order without ever driving more lamps than the breaker allows.
Four training buttons rather than three because three buttons have exactly three pairs: a three-lamp board would use them all and no button setting could light two lamps without the third.

## Annotated videos

All four clips run on the same five-button, four-lamp test board, so any difference between them is attributable to the policy rather than the board.
That board's wiring is `yellow lamp0 <- blue button2 AND orange button3`, `cyan lamp1 <- red button0 AND green button1`, `magenta lamp2 <- red button0 AND blue button2`, `white lamp3 <- green button1 AND purple button4`.
The blue button is shared between the yellow and magenta lamps, the red button between the cyan and magenta lamps, and the green button between the cyan and white lamps.
The goal asks for the cyan and magenta lamps lit and the yellow and white lamps dark.

Each frame pairs the camera view an agent actually gets with a panel showing the board's hidden state - the true wiring and every lamp's latent charge.
The red mark on each charge bar is the brightness onset; left of it the lamp looks completely dead.

### Outcomes

- `oracle_solve.mp4` (12 s) - press the red, green and blue buttons, then wait. Solves the task.
- `press_everything_fails.mp4` (13 s) - latch every button and wait, the policy an agent falls into once it knows buttons matter but not which. It lights all four lamps, including the yellow and white ones, and fails the off-targets.

The second clip is the reason goals require some lamp to stay dark.
Without an off-target, latching every button would solve every task without knowing anything about the board.

### Mechanism

These two show why the board is hard, rather than only what happens on it.

- `interlock.mp4` (20 s) - press the orange button alone, one of the two buttons the yellow lamp needs, and wait. Nothing happens for ten full seconds, and the charge bar makes clear it is not a slow response: the bar never leaves zero, because the drive condition was never met. Press the blue button as well and the charge climbs and the lamp lights. This conjunction is the many-to-one relation that prior busyboard environments for robot learning exclude by design.
- `delayed_credit.mp4` (17 s) - the confound. The blue and orange buttons are pressed, so the yellow lamp begins charging invisibly; then the unrelated green button is pressed, and the yellow lamp lights part-way through that press. The button that appears to cause the effect is not the button that caused it, and the charge bar is the only thing in the frame that says so. This is the mis-attribution a press-and-look agent makes, and the reason exploration here has to be designed rather than merely thorough.

## Goal distribution

A goal is drawn uniformly from the lamp assignments some button setting realizes, minus "nothing lit" and "everything lit", with only training lamps as lit candidates and at least `busyboard_min_lit_<split>` lamps lit.
For seed 0 the training board has 5 realizable goals (3 single-lamp, 2 two-lamp) and each test board has 3 (2 two-lamp, 1 three-lamp).
The render script prints both a 120-task sample per split and these exact counts.
